|Other<br>considerations|Royal College of<br>Pathologists and<br>BSH|Currently, many authorities believe that there should be three approaches to<br>the first-line treatment of CLL depending on fitness and the presence of a p53<br>deletion. Although it has never been proved in a randomised trial (and is<br>unlikely ever to be), patients with 17p- (5-10% of cases) are thought to benefit<br>from alemtuzumab-based therapy in preference to a chemotherapy-based<br>approach. For the majority of patients who do not have a p53 defect, R-FC is<br>now considered the treatment of choice for fit patients, while chlorambucil<br>(Chl) still remains the standard of care for less fit patients who are unable to<br>tolerate R-FC.<br>It is not entirely clear where bendamustine might fit into this framework. Thus,<br>although it might appear to be more effective than Chl, it is also more toxic<br>(grade 3-4 toxicity 40% versus 19% in the phase III RCT comparing it with<br>Chl), and it is therefore unclear how well it would be tolerated by less fit<br>patients who are unable to tolerate R-FC.<br>There are also questions to ask about the efficacy of bendamustine since the<br>median PFS in the phase III RCT comparing it with Chl was not particularly<br>impressive (21.6 months) - strikingly similar to the the median PFS observed<br>in the Chl arm of the UK CLL4 trial (20 months) and considerably shorter than<br>the median PFS in the FC arm of the UK CLL4 trial (43 months). Importantly,<br>the Chl regimen employed in the bendamustine trial was somewhat<br>unconventional (at least for UK practice) and involved administering a<br>relatively big dose on day 1 and 15 of each cycle rather than a more modest<br>dose over 7 consecutive days as in the UK CLL4 trial.
