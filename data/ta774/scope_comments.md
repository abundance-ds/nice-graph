|Population|Celgene|Broadly, yes – the current definition reflects  the anticipated indication for this<br>technology.<br>However, as indicated in the Background section – and our comments above<br>- there will be distinct patient sub-groups and treatment pathways that can be<br>defined, and which should be considered separately.<br>MCL is a very heterogeneous condition ranging from indolent to highly<br>aggressive disease. A primary distinction can be made according to the<br>overall aim of therapy, i.e.; either to get eligible patients to stem cell<br>transplant (SCT), or achieve extended remission, or palliation. Tumour load,<br>plus patient age and frailty determine treatment choice/intensity, and thus<br>which of these therapeutic aims are viable. So in general, more intense<br>therapeutic regimens or monotherapies are selected when possible (e.g.;<br>fitter patients, pre-SCT) and less intense regimens otherwise (e.g.; less fit<br>patients, to extend remission or palliate). At this later stage of the pathway<br>there are limited proven and licensed treatment options and there is no clear<br>standard of care.<br>Lenalidomide should not be considered to fall within the category of ‘intense’<br>chemotherapies. The profile and expected clinical performance of<br>lenalidomide has allowed clinical opinion leaders to predict the relative<br>effectiveness – and thus place in therapy – of this technology. The pivotal<br>clinical trial population were patients ineligible for SCT and intensive<br>chemotherapy, and clinicians have indicated that they would not position<br>lenalidomide for use in these SCT/intense therapy patients. Instead, they<br>have advised that the optimal position for lenalidomide is likely to be in<br>patients who are ineligible for intensive or combination chemotherapy (R-<br>CHOP), or bendamustine+rituximab.|Comment noted. The<br>population is in line with<br>the expected marketing<br>authorisation. No<br>change to scope<br>required.|
