|Background<br>information|Aplastic Anaemia Trust|Aplastic anaemia (AA) is a rare illness of the immune system,<br>whereby the bone marrow fails, destroying stem cells, thus halting<br>the production of vital red, white blood cells and platelets. This<br>affects people’s ability to function – they may bleed<br>simultaneously, bruise, attract life-threatening infections and are<br>haunted by extreme fatigue. If untreated, people die quickly.<br>Treatment closely resembles that of cancer patients, and will be<br>prolonged, costly and with severe side effects in a lot of patients.<br>The only curative treatment currently is a stem cell transplant,<br>however, this will not be a suitable treatment option for a large<br>proportion of patients. Aplastic anaemia is intrinsically linked to<br>Paroxysmal Nocturnal Haemoglobinuria (PNH). There is strong<br>evidence to indicate that all patients with PNH have an underlying<br>bone marrow failure, usually aplastic anaemia, either preceding or<br>coexistent with the diagnosis of PNH (Rotoli et al, 1982; Rotoli &<br>Luzzatto, 1989; Maciejewski et al, 1997). Whilst the ‘PNH clone’<br>does not have any ‘malignant’ tendency, however, it expands if<br>there is a selective advantage for it to do so (Rawstron et al,<br>1999) as described in aplastic anaemia patients that develop PNH<br>(Tichelli et al, 1988; Schrezenmeier et al, 1995).<br>“PNH only arises in the background of bone marrow failure<br>syndromes, the most common being aplastic anaemia. That<br>means that these two diseases are very closely linked. In fact, you<br>cannot have PNH without having AA. Both can be very severe<br>and life threatening with huge mortality. Supporting patients<br>undergoing treatment for both illnesses is therefore crucial.“ Dr<br>Anita Hill, Lead Consultant for National PNH Service, Leeds,<br>Englandcom|Comment noted. The<br>background section of the<br>scope is only intended to<br>briefly describe the disease in<br>the remit, prognosis<br>associated with the condition,<br>epidemiology and treatments<br>currently used in the NHS. No<br>change to the scope required.|
