||Society of<br>British<br>Neurological<br>Surgeons<br>(SBNS)|1.  With respect to the accuracy and completeness of the background<br>information one of the major issues is definition of recurrence and how this is<br>achieved.  Patients are frequently diagnosed with recurrent disease based on<br>clinic deterioration but increasingly the evidence is that early detection by<br>imaging can be more beneficial.  In the context of regular surveillance imaging,<br>early recurrence is associated with improved performance and greater<br>likelihood of access to therapy for recurrence.  Many older studies have used<br>symptomatic recurrence where inherently the performance level of these<br>patients at recurrence is worse and hence debars patients from either trial<br>entry or expectant treatments.  Current treatment should try and maximise the<br>number of patients in whom it should be useful.  Imaging surveillance from<br>diagnosis and glioblastoma multiforme especially should be regular and<br>consistent as patients can recur even after prolonged periods of stability.<br>Therefore, imaging should be at between eight weeks to twelve weeks to<br>enable us the greatest chance of picking up recurrence.<br>This is reflected in the recently published Improving Outcomes Guidance which<br>pays particular emphasis to follow-up surveillance.<br>The appraisal should consider the impact of the IOG and definition of<br>recurrence in its deliberations, bearing in mind that image-based deterioration<br>is increasingly the standard by which these patients are managed worldwide.<br>2.  It is incorrect to use a unimodal model defining disease progression in these<br>patients whereby they would have a disease-free interval and then deteriorate<br>to death.  Patients with glioblastoma multiforme will always have the disease<br>which can wax and wane during its natural history, sometimes as a result of<br>treatment.  Frequently patients will have recurrence based on imaging (or<br>symptoms) which responds to chemotherapy +/- surgery to give prolonged<br>periods of disease control, e.g. greater than a year.  Indeed, we have a number<br>of patients from the Queen Elizabeth Hospital series who have had several<br>years of good performance level following from apparent recurrence.  Unimodal<br>models of the disease are inadequate to describe uses of treatment, cost<br>benefit, and quality of life.<br>The appraisal should take these issues of disease behaviour into account in<br>any modelling process.|1. The potential importance of<br>early detection of recurrence<br>is noted. ||SBNS|[1.]  The description of the technology is basically correct but it should be<br>emphasised that it is a surgical treatment with the Gliadel used as an adjunct to<br>surgery to improve effects.  The evidence from Brem, Westphal, and more<br>recently from Rikken and others is that this technology is able to realise its<br>effect best where maximal resection (80-90%) is possible.  This means that<br>there will inevitably be some degree of selection of patients to affect the best<br>results.  It also means that a number of patients will not be eligible for<br>treatment.  Recent estimates suggest this may be of the order of around about<br>20-30% of patients at recurrence.  In addition, the surgeon must be able to<br>achieve a watertight closure of the dural layer.<br>2.  The technology is quite different to conventional chemotherapy in that once<br>placed in the patient’s intracranial environment does not require bone marrow<br>monitoring or support as there is no evidence of impact on bone marrow from<br>the treatment placed in the intracranial cavity.|1.
